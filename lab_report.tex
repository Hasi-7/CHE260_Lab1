\section{Introduction}

\subsection{Ideal Gas Law}

Gases are a fundamental foundation for understanding chemistry
and the quantitative laws of their behavior provide us with a
direct connection between macroscopic and particle level
properties. A principal example of this is the Ideal Gas Law.
The Law was first derived in 1834 by Benoît Paul Émile Clapeyron
who combined several empirical laws including Boyle's Law and
Charles's Law [1]. The most common way to write this law is:

\begin{equation}
    PV = NR_uT
\end{equation}

Where $P$ is the pressure measured in the system, for this
experiment the Pressure value is calculated as
$P = P_{\mathrm{gauge}} + P_{\mathrm{atm}}$ where
$P_{\mathrm{gauge}}$ is the pressure measured on the gauge
and $P_{\mathrm{atm}}$ is the atmospheric pressure of the
room the experiment took place in, $V$ is the volume of the
system, $N$ is the amount of moles of gas present in the
system, $R_u$ is the gas constant,
$8.31.4~\mathrm{J\,mol^{-1}K^{-1}}$ and finally $T$
is the temperature of the system expressed in Kelvin.
By understanding that $N$ is the quotient of the total mass
of the gas in the system and the molar mass of the gas(s),
then the formula can be rearranged to

\begin{equation}
    PV = mRT
\end{equation}

Where the new $R$ constant is the gas constant $R_u$
divided by the molar mass of the gas. For Air this value
is $287~\mathrm{J/(kg\cdot K)}$ [2]. This is important for
this experiment as the mass of the air can be calculated
through the measured mass flow rate. In an ``ideal gas''
as the law describes, particles move stochastically and
independently of each other, only interacting through
elastic collisions which conserve both momentum and
kinetic energy [3].

\subsection{Thermodynamic State Properties and Path Independence}

A Thermodynamic property is something which affects the
energy of the system that can be measured without knowing
the previous state of the system [4]. These properties
are used to describe the thermodynamic state of the system,
these include pressure, temperature, volume, mass, and
density. The properties listed also only depend on the
current state of the system, these are called state
properties and the principle is known as path independence.

This concept is especially important for this lab, as
the first part compares two different methods of equalizing
pressure. In the first method, air is moved rapidly through a solenoid valve,
while in the second method, air is transferred gradually
through a needle valve. While intermidate sections of the processes
may vary, both methods are expected to produce the same final equilibrium
conditions.

\subsection{Heat Transfer and the First Law of Thermodynamics}

This is described through the First Law of Thermodynamics
which states that energy is the change in internal energy
of a closed system is equal to the heat transferred into
the system subtracted by the work done by the system [4].
Because the total volume of the system in the lab is
constant the change in internal energy is equivalent
to the net heat transfer.

\subsection{Air Compressibility}

Finally another important characteristic of gases is
their compressibility which describes how their volume
or density changes in response to variations in pressure.
Unlike liquids and solids, gases are highly compressible
due to the large distance between molecules [5].

In this experiment the two tanks have a constant volume
throughout the process. Because of this the changes in
pressure are related to the changes in mass, density,
and the temperature of the air in each tank. This
relationship can be rearranged to be

\begin{equation}
    \rho = \frac{P}{RT}
\end{equation}


However, the Ideal Gas Law assumes that the volume
occupied by individual gas molecules and the
intermolecular forces between them are negligible.

\subsubsection{Heat Transfer and Net Heat Transfer}

Though heat transfer is a path-dependent quantity, the net
heat transfer in both expansion cases is roughly the same
because the initial and final thermodynamic states are
approximately identical. However, the heat transfer histories
differ significantly between the two processes.

During Part 1A, rapid expansion, once the solenoid valve is
opened, the temperature in the left tank experiences a sharp
decrease while the temperature of the right tank suddenly
increases, before both temperatures slowly reach thermal
equilibrium. This sharp change in temperature occurs because
the flow of air between the tanks is faster than the rate at
which heat can be exchanged with the surroundings.

Meanwhile, in the slow expansion experiment, once the needle
valve is opened, the temperatures of the two tanks gradually
reach thermal equilibrium over a much longer period of time,
without sudden changes in temperature.

When determining the net heat transfer, both tanks can be
considered as a single closed system. Because the air
initially and finally reaches approximately ambient
temperature, there is no boundary work, and the total mass
of the system remains constant, the First Law of
Thermodynamics gives:

\begin{equation}
    \Delta U = Q - W
\end{equation}

Since the tanks are rigid, no boundary work is performed,
and assuming negligible other work interactions:

\begin{equation}
    W = 0
\end{equation}

Additionally, since the initial and final temperatures
are roughly equal, the change in internal energy
is approximately zero:

\begin{equation}
    \Delta U \approx 0
\end{equation}

Therefore, the net heat transfer is:

\begin{equation}
    \boxed{Q_{\mathrm{net}} \approx 0}
\end{equation}

\subsubsection{Path Independence of State Properties}
% Part 1, Question 3: compare the two paths and their final states.
% Include and reference a figure supporting the explanation.
As mentioned previously, a thermodynamic state property is a
property whose value depends only on the current state of the
system and not on the process used to reach that state. The
experiment demonstrates the path independence of state
properties by comparing two different methods of pressure
equalization between interconnected tanks.

In Part 1A, air is transferred rapidly through a solenoid
valve, leading to sudden changes in pressure and temperature,
as seen in Figure~\ref{fig:part1a}. Alternatively, in
Part 1B, air is transferred gradually through a needle
valve, producing slower changes in these properties, as
seen in Figure~\ref{fig:part1b}.

Despite these differences in the paths of the two processes,
the final equilibrium pressure is approximately the same
when starting from equivalent initial conditions. Therefore,
the experiment illustrates how different processes can
connect the same initial and final equilibrium states,
while thermodynamic state properties remain independent
of the path taken between them.

\subsubsection{Effect of Air Compressibility}

The compressibility of air can greatly affect experimental results of pressure and volume, especially as expiremental conditions diverge from assumptions made by the ideal gas equation. The ideal gas equation assumes that temperatures are kept high and pressures are kept low. When pressures are kept low, intermolecular forces can be assumed to be negligible. With this model, the compressibility of air can be modelled to be constant. As the pressure increases, electromagnetic repulsions and attractions between particles greatly contribute to the behavior of the gas. This causes the linear model of air compressibility to break down, displaying the inaccuracies in the ideal gas model. Since the intermolecular forces increase as pressure increases, our calculated compressibility is much higher than the actual compressibility.

One can model this using the compressibility factor, where
\[
    Z = \frac{Pv}{RT}
\]
or
\[
    PV - ZRT.
\]
Under constant volume, this measures the divergence of a physical model from Gay-Lussac's Law.

During our expirementation, the maximum gauge pressure found was around 40 psi. Given that this is a similar gauge pressure to that seen in pressurized car tire, this is high enough where our apparatus diverges from assumptions made by the ideal gas model and we should check the compressibility factor during our process.

If we hold the volume \(V\) constant, we can calculate the compressibility factors at low and high pressures.

At \(t = 10\):
\begin{align*}
    1.3 \cdot v &= Z_1 \cdot R \cdot 317.85, \\
    Z_1 &= \frac{1.3 \cdot v}{317.85 \cdot R}
        = \frac{2}{489} \cdot \frac{v}{R}.
\end{align*}

At \(t = 50\):
\begin{align*}
    52.2 \cdot v &= Z_2 \cdot R \cdot 301.45, \\
    Z_1 &= \frac{52.2 \cdot v}{301.45 \cdot R}
        = \frac{1050}{6029} \cdot \frac{v}{R}.
\end{align*}

Thus, through these calculations, we can see that the compressibility of air significantly increases at higher pressures.
