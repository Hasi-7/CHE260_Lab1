\section{Introduction}

\subsection{Ideal Gas Law}

Gases provide a fundamental foundation for understanding
chemistry, and the quantitative laws governing their behavior
establish a direct connection between macroscopic and
particle-level properties. A principal example of this
relationship is the Ideal Gas Law. The law was formulated
in 1834 by Benoît Paul Émile Clapeyron, who combined
empirical relationships including Boyle's Law and
Charles's Law [1]. The most common formulation is:

\begin{equation}
    PV = NR_uT
\end{equation}

where $P$ is the absolute pressure of the system, $V$ is
the volume, $N$ is the number of moles of gas, $R_u$ is
the universal gas constant
($8.314~\mathrm{J/(mol\cdot K)}$), and $T$ is the
absolute temperature expressed in Kelvin.

For this experiment, the absolute pressure is calculated
using:

\begin{equation}
    P_{\mathrm{abs}} =
    P_{\mathrm{gauge}} + P_{\mathrm{atm}}
\end{equation}

where $P_{\mathrm{gauge}}$ is the pressure measured by
the gauge and $P_{\mathrm{atm}}$ is the atmospheric
pressure measured in the laboratory.

Since the number of moles is equal to the total mass of
the gas divided by its molar mass, the Ideal Gas Law
can be rearranged as:

\begin{equation}
    PV = mRT
\end{equation}

where $m$ is the mass of the gas and $R$ is the
specific gas constant, obtained by dividing $R_u$ by
the molar mass. For air, this value is approximately
$287~\mathrm{J/(kg\cdot K)}$ [2].

This formulation is particularly important for this
experiment because the mass of air introduced into the
tank can be calculated from the measured mass flow rate.

Under the ideal gas model, particles are assumed to
move randomly and independently, with negligible
molecular volume and negligible intermolecular forces
except during collisions. These collisions are assumed
to be perfectly elastic, conserving both momentum
and kinetic energy [3].

\subsection{Thermodynamic State Properties and Path Independence}

To properly understand the Ideal Gas Law, it is also
important to understand thermodynamic properties,
particularly state properties. A thermodynamic property
is a measurable or calculable macroscopic characteristic
of a system whose value can be determined without
knowing the previous history of the system [4].

These properties describe the thermodynamic state of
a system and include pressure, temperature, volume,
mass, and density. Their values depend only on the
current state of the system rather than the path taken
to reach that state. This characteristic is known as
path independence.

This concept is especially important for this laboratory
experiment, as the first part compares two different
methods of equalizing pressure between interconnected
tanks of air.

In the first method, air is transferred rapidly through
a solenoid valve, while in the second method, air is
transferred gradually through a needle valve. Although
the pressure and temperature histories may differ between
these processes, both methods are expected to produce
approximately the same final equilibrium conditions,
provided the initial conditions and final constraints
are equivalent.

Path independence can also be demonstrated through
the change in internal energy of a system:

\begin{equation}
    \Delta U = U_f - U_i
\end{equation}

where $U_f$ and $U_i$ represent the final and initial
internal energies, respectively. Since internal energy
is a state property, its change depends only on the
initial and final thermodynamic states.

This differs from heat transfer and work, which are
path-dependent quantities.

\subsection{Heat Transfer and the First Law of Thermodynamics}

An important consideration when studying gas expansion
is the transfer of energy between a system and its
surroundings. This relationship is described by the
First Law of Thermodynamics, which states that energy
is conserved [4].

For a closed system, neglecting changes in kinetic
and potential energy, the First Law can be written as:

\begin{equation}
    \Delta U = Q - W
\end{equation}

where $\Delta U$ is the change in internal energy,
$Q$ is the net heat transferred into the system,
and $W$ is the work done by the system.

In this experiment, the two interconnected rigid tanks
can be treated as a single closed system. Since the
total volume remains constant, there is no boundary
work. Assuming negligible other work interactions,
the First Law becomes:

\begin{equation}
    \Delta U = Q
\end{equation}

Heat transfer is particularly relevant when comparing
the rapid and gradual pressure-equalization methods.
During rapid expansion through the solenoid valve,
air moves between the tanks over a relatively short
period, limiting the time available for heat exchange
with the surroundings.

Consequently, temporary temperature differences may
develop between the two tanks.

In contrast, gradual expansion through the needle
valve provides more time for heat exchange with the
surrounding environment, allowing the system to
remain closer to thermal equilibrium throughout the
process.

Although the two methods may produce different
temperature histories, they are expected to approach
similar final equilibrium conditions.

\subsection{Air Compressibility and Intermolecular Forces}

Finally, another important characteristic of gases
is their compressibility, which describes how their
volume or density changes in response to variations
in pressure.

Unlike liquids and solids, gases are highly
compressible due to the relatively large distances
between their molecules [5].

In this experiment, the two tanks are rigid, meaning
their volumes remain constant throughout the process.
Consequently, changes in pressure are associated
with changes in the mass, density, and temperature
of the air within each tank.

This relationship can be described using the
Ideal Gas Law, rearranged as:

\begin{equation}
    \rho = \frac{P}{RT}
\end{equation}

where $\rho$ represents the density of air.

However, the Ideal Gas Law assumes that the volume
occupied by individual gas molecules and the
intermolecular forces between them are negligible.

In reality, attractive and repulsive intermolecular
forces influence gas behavior, particularly at high
pressures or low temperatures. As a result, real gases
may deviate from the predictions of the Ideal Gas Law.

These deviations can be quantified using the
compressibility factor, $Z$:

\begin{equation}
    Z = \frac{Pv}{RT}
\end{equation}

where $v$ represents the specific volume of the gas.

For an ideal gas, $Z = 1$, while values different
from unity indicate deviations from ideal gas behavior.

In this experiment, the maximum gauge pressure was
approximately $40~\mathrm{psi}$, corresponding to
an absolute pressure of approximately
$3.8~\mathrm{bar}$.

Under these relatively moderate conditions, air is
expected to behave approximately as an ideal gas,
with a compressibility factor close to unity.

Therefore, although intermolecular forces may
introduce some uncertainty into the calculated
tank volumes, their effects are expected to be
relatively small.

\subsubsection{Effect of Air Compressibility}

The compressibility of air can greatly affect experimental results of pressure and volume, especially as expiremental conditions diverge from assumptions made by the ideal gas equation. The ideal gas equation assumes that temperatures are kept high and pressures are kept low. When pressures are kept low, intermolecular forces can be assumed to be negligible. With this model, the compressibility of air can be modelled to be constant. As the pressure increases, electromagnetic repulsions and attractions between particles greatly contribute to the behavior of the gas. This causes the linear model of air compressibility to break down, displaying the inaccuracies in the ideal gas model. Since the intermolecular forces increase as pressure increases, our calculated compressibility is much higher than the actual compressibility.

One can model this using the compressibility factor, where
\[
    Z = \frac{Pv}{RT}
\]
or
\[
    PV - ZRT.
\]
Under constant volume, this measures the divergence of a physical model from Gay-Lussac's Law.

During our expirementation, the maximum gauge pressure found was around 40 psi. Given that this is a similar gauge pressure to that seen in pressurized car tire, this is high enough where our apparatus diverges from assumptions made by the ideal gas model and we should check the compressibility factor during our process.

If we hold the volume \(V\) constant, we can calculate the compressibility factors at low and high pressures.

At \(t = 10\):
\begin{align*}
    1.3 \cdot v &= Z_1 \cdot R \cdot 317.85, \\
    Z_1 &= \frac{1.3 \cdot v}{317.85 \cdot R}
        = \frac{2}{489} \cdot \frac{v}{R}.
\end{align*}

At \(t = 50\):
\begin{align*}
    52.2 \cdot v &= Z_2 \cdot R \cdot 301.45, \\
    Z_1 &= \frac{52.2 \cdot v}{301.45 \cdot R}
        = \frac{1050}{6029} \cdot \frac{v}{R}.
\end{align*}

Thus, through these calculations, we can see that the compressibility of air significantly increases at higher pressures.
